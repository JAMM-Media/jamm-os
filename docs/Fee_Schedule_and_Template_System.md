## 0. What changed since the August 12 version, in one paragraph
Three things. First, the depth-two nesting limit is gone: firms can now chain pricing dimensions to arbitrary depth, with rules that keep it safe and fillable (section 4 explains the model and the reasoning, because the reasoning is what future sessions need to preserve). Second, the system complexity catalog is stored as global shared tables with a documented exception to the firm_id rule (section 4.1 explains why per-firm copies were considered and rejected). Third, a set of facts got settled by the build itself: catalog entries are created lazily rather than backfilled, blank and zero prices are physically distinct in the schema, the Postgres floor is now 15 (production and CI run 18), and one known structural gap (categorical branch ambiguity) is guarded at save time with its durable fix on the deferred ledger.

## 3. Service catalog: single source of truth
One catalog entry per firm per engagement type, unique on (firm_id, engagement_type). The entry carries offered-or-not, the pricing mode, and the base fee, and in Build 2 it gains the template binding. The form, the automation, and the Concierge checklist all read this one state.
Rows are created lazily, and this matters enough to spell out. There is no backfill of 43 catalog rows per firm. The absence of a row means not offered, and it means exactly the same thing as a row explicitly marked not offered. A row comes into existence the first time a firm activates (or touches) that service. The reasoning: every type defaults to not offered anyway, so 43 dormant rows per firm carry zero information, cost a backfill script on every firm creation and every future type addition, and create thousands of dead rows that audits and queries wade through. Lazy creation gets identical semantics for free. Important disambiguation: the August 12 document's line about "the backfill creates dormant presets for all 43 types" was about Build 2's letter templates, where a guaranteed-present row per type IS required (Ben's proposal lookup must never miss). Templates get the backfill; catalog entries do not. Do not conflate the two.
The activation law. Turning a service on requires a pricing mode chosen in the same operation, and (from Build 2 onward) a template confirmed. A service cannot be half-on. This is enforced in the service layer today and guard-tested; the refusal leaves no partial row behind. The reason is Ben's side of the system: his form advertises whatever is active, and an active service with no pricing mode would give the automation an instruction it cannot follow.
Choosing offered services is an onboarding step the Concierge drives, and unconfigured surface area (an activated service with no base fee, a crypto flag live for 30 days with no tiers priced) is a standing Concierge signal, not a one-time check.

## 4. The pricing model
This section is a rewrite, because the model changed on August 13 and the reasoning behind the change is part of the specification. Future sessions should preserve the reasoning, not just the rules, because the rules only stay coherent if you know what they protect.
### 4.1 Who owns what: the carve-out
The catalog of complexity content (flags like crypto and K-1s, their dimensions like type and volume, the unit menus, the answer vocabularies) is system-owned shared data in global tables that carry no firm_id. This is a documented exception to the standing rule that every table carries firm_id, ruled August 13. Everything financial (which services a firm offers, which dimensions it configured, its tiers, its prices, its guards) lives in firm-scoped tables where tenant isolation applies absolutely, as everywhere else.
Why global rather than per-firm copies (the template precedent): per-firm copies exist to let content diverge, and this catalog is forbidden from diverging. Firms attach prices; they never author lead-facing text. Copying rows no firm may edit buys nothing and costs a sync obligation forever: every wording correction Lori or a founding firm surfaces becomes a sweep script across every firm instead of a one-row edit, and every content-session addition becomes a backfill. Global tables also give cross-firm intelligence shared option identity for free: when two firms' leads both answer "staking," it is literally the same option row. The per-firm state that matters (prices, tiers, directions, roles) is firm-scoped in both designs, so isolation of anything financial is identical either way. If firm-authored custom flags ever arrive (deferred ledger), they will live in a separate per-firm table beside the global one; substitution, never blending, applied to table design.
The nine shipped tables: complexity_flags, complexity_flag_engagement_types, complexity_dimensions, complexity_dimension_units, complexity_vocabulary_options (system-owned, no firm_id); service_catalog_entries, firm_dimension_configs, firm_tiers, firm_option_prices (firm-scoped).
### 4.2 Dimension kinds and roles (unchanged from v1)
Three kinds: boolean (exists or not, flat adder, no follow-up), numeric range (the firm tiers on a system-preset unit chosen from a menu, e.g. transaction count vs total proceeds vs account count), categorical (system-preset vocabulary, the firm prices the options it covers, and every categorical question carries an Other option).
Three roles a firm assigns when configuring a dimension: priced (the answer moves the price), informational (asked and captured, no price effect; arms the firm owner for the call and feeds the behavioral log), guard (a threshold on any dimension that overrides everything and routes to quote when crossed; requires a threshold value).
### 4.3 The direction choice: flat or dependent
Every dimension a firm configures gets a direction. Either it is a separate fee: independent, carries its own tier prices (or option prices), and stacks additively with everything else. Or it is dependent: it hangs under a parent (a specific numeric tier or a specific categorical option of a coarser dimension), and the pricing moves to the combination. Chains can go to arbitrary depth. This choice is made per dimension, per flag, per firm; it is not a firm-wide mode, and the expectation is that no firm applies one style universally. The same firm will chain crypto because their pricing there is dialed, run K-1s as three flat stacked fees, and leave rental gross rents informational.
Why the August 12 depth-two limit existed, and why it fell. The limit was protection against a combinatorial wall: forced matrices no firm owner would ever fill out. Two things dissolved the fear. First, the universal quote law (4.6) already rescues every unfilled cell: unpriced paths route to quote automatically, so a firm prices only the branches they care about and dormant depth is free, exactly like dormant vocabulary options. Second, the settings UI is progressive: a firm starts with one question and an "add a tier below this" action, so depth is something a firm walks into voluntarily, never a form they confront. With both in place, the limit was protecting against a problem that no longer exists, while blocking firms who genuinely price this way. The timing also mattered: this was changed at schema design time, before any migration existed, when it was cheap. Changing it after launch would have been a painful refactor.
How real firms actually price, and why both shapes are needed. A firm most often prices crypto as separate flat fees: something per account reconciled, something per transaction volume bracket, summed. That is the flat model, and it is the default. But some firms want the combination priced directly (2 accounts at 1k-20k transactions is one number, 10 accounts at the same volume is another), and for them the dependency chain expresses it exactly. The interaction cases a flat model cannot express and a firm does not want to matrix (e.g. absurd volume in few accounts) are what guards are for: threshold crossed, route to quote, price it by hand.
Money lives at the end of a chain: the no-double-counting rule. In a dependent chain, only the leaf carries prices, one number per cell. A parent tier or parent option that has children is structural and carries no price, and the service layer refuses both directions of violation (pricing a tier that has children; hanging a child under a priced tier). The reason: if the parent kept a price AND the cell had a price, the firm would pay for accounts twice, once in the account adder and again baked into the cell. Leaf-only is the only version with no double counting. Flat dimensions each carry their own prices, because each flat dimension is a chain of length one. Resolution then sums: base fee, plus the categorical option adder, plus each chain walked to its one leaf number, plus each flat layer's adder. One total, or quote. No weighting, no percentages, no judgment about which dimension matters more, because each level already said what it is worth in dollars.
Dependent tiers are branch-specific by construction. A child hanging under accounts tier 1 can carry different tiers and prices than the same dimension under accounts tier 3, because each cell belongs to its branch. The builder UI provides a copy-to-other-branches convenience so the common case (same structure everywhere) feels like defining it once.
### 4.4 The system owns the hierarchy; firms only choose whether to link
Every dimension within a flag carries a system-assigned hierarchy rank, coarse to fine (accounts before transactions before dollar volume; property count before gross rents). Chains only ever flow downhill: a dimension can hang only under a strictly coarser one within the same flag, enforced at save time. Firms never pick a parent from a menu and never reorder the hierarchy; when they choose "dependent," the parent is the nearest coarser dimension they actually configured. A firm that skips a dimension simply drops it out of the chain (skip accounts, and transactions becomes the top of its own chain; no orphan, no error).
Why: a free direction choice would allow nonsensical trees (transactions as the parent of accounts), which produce garbage pricing structure and garbage cross-firm data. System-owned ordering makes the builder UI dumber in the best way (no way to build a backwards tree), and makes every firm that chains a flag chain it in the same direction, so the Intelligence Index compares like with like structurally. The split is the same one running through the whole design: structure is system-owned, prices are firm-owned.
Dimensions with no sensible mutual order are marked unlinkable in the catalog and never offered as dependencies. Assigning ranks and linkable flags across the roughly 45 flags is a content-session deliverable (section 7); most are obvious, the arguable ones get a call that Lori sanity-checks and founding firms correct as data edits.
### 4.5 Tier mechanics and the null-versus-zero law
Numeric tiers must be contiguous and complete, per dimension per branch: no gaps, no overlaps, at most one open-top tier and it must be last, enforced at save time with errors that name the offending boundary. Firms price bounded ranges only; the open top is automatically quote territory and cannot carry a price. Lead-facing option labels are generated from the firm's numbers: firms input digits, the system writes the words.
The null-versus-zero law, now physical in the schema. A blank price (NULL, no default anywhere) means unpriced, and routes to quote under the one law. An explicit zero means priced at zero: the firm charges nothing for this, deliberately. These are different facts and are never coerced into each other, in either direction, at any layer (model, migration, schema, service). This distinction is what the old JSON blob could not express (unpriced and unoffered were indistinguishable by construction), and it is guard-tested including the realistic collapse (defaulting null to zero on insert).
Changing a dimension's direction is explicit and destructive. Moving a dimension between flat and dependent, or between branches, invalidates every price below it (the numbers stop meaning what they meant). So it is a dedicated confirmed action: warned, confirmed with an explicit flag, audited, event-logged, and it deletes every tier and option price belonging to the moved dimension and its descendants. Blanks route to quote until refilled. No other code path may alter parent references, and no silent restructuring exists anywhere. Related, measured during the build: a routine tier edit can never restructure or destroy a subtree; edits are applied in place, and removing a tier that still has children is refused and pointed at the direction-change action.
### 4.6 The universal quote law
Any lead answer landing on anything without a price attached routes to needs-a-quote. That covers: the open top range, numbers above the firm's highest priced tier, the Other option on every categorical question, unpriced vocabulary options, unpriced cells and branches at any depth of a chain, and crossed guards. One law, one enforcement point in backend fee resolution, no silent failures and no mispricing. This law is also the structural reason arbitrary depth is safe: nothing a firm leaves unfilled can ever produce a wrong number, only a routed-to-human one. The system never quietly guesses; it either computes from what the firm consciously priced, or it hands the lead to the firm owner.
### 4.7 Fee resolution (backend only)
At proposal time: base fee, plus the categorical option adder, plus each dependent chain resolved by walking the lead's raw answers through the firm's branches to a single leaf number, plus each additional flat priced layer's adder. Guards override the sum to quote. Resolution lives in the backend exclusively; the frontend send modal's client-side fee math is never used by the automated path and is a retirement candidate once the config endpoint and backend resolution are live. A template using {{fee_amount}} with no pricing row for that type fails send validation: no send, firm owner notified with the missing fields.
### 4.8 Known structural gap, guarded: categorical branch ambiguity
Because a child config references its parent vocabulary option alone (options belong to the system dimension, not to any one firm config of it), a categorical dimension configured on two branches with option-parented children underneath would leave those children unable to name their branch, and a direction change on either branch would delete prices belonging to the other. A save-time rule (rule 8 in the validation service, guard-tested in both directions) refuses creating that shape. The durable fix, a parent_config_id so a child names its branch outright, is a ledgered schema change; when it lands, the save-time rule retires and the one gap it does not cover (a direction change moving a config INTO an ambiguous arrangement) closes with it.

## 5. Public config endpoint (next session)
Unauthenticated, per firm, rate limited like the other public surfaces. Serves: active engagement types with display labels; per type, the applicable complexity questions with system-written question text; per categorical dimension, the vocabulary options with opaque IDs; per numeric dimension, the question generated from the firm's chosen unit. Never serves prices, adders, guards, tier boundaries, or the fee schedule itself, and never exposes the firm's chain structure. Depth is invisible to the form: when a flag applies, the firm's configured dimensions for it are all served as ordinary questions regardless of how the firm chained them underneath, because chains shape pricing, not question visibility.
Numeric questions capture the raw number from the lead; the server maps it to the firm's tiers at resolution time. Raw values persist in the behavioral log forever, so cross-firm intelligence compares real quantities regardless of how any firm cut its tiers. Categorical answers come back as canonical option IDs.
Required items for the endpoint session, carried from Build 1 Session 2: RBAC at every endpoint (the pricing service currently has no HTTP surface, so the standing rule has had nothing to attach to; this session is where it attaches), and the guard test that watches the enrollment partial unique index against a migrated database (the one destructive item in the 23-item drift inventory, currently unwatched).

## 6. Template system (Build 2)
Unchanged from v1. Per-firm copies with preset_key lineage, following the automation rules precedent exactly: no shared rows, no nullable firm_id, tenant isolation untouched. Guaranteed present by data migration backfill plus the firm-creation seed service (this is the backfill the catalog does not have, and needs it because Ben's proposal lookup is "engagement type in, template out" with no fallback). Immutable versioning: every edit writes a new version, version 1 is the pristine preset body, revert creates a new version equal to version 1, is_customized flips once and stays flipped. Seed consolidation: firm-creation seed service becomes the single source, the two conflicting standalone scripts retire, the tax_return_990 orphan resolves via the enum expansion. engagement_type validated against the real enum at the schema layer (the pattern now shipped on the catalog tables). ## 10. Why this design holds up, in plain terms
For any reader (Ben especially) wanting the one-paragraph version of why this is safe to build on: the system can never produce a wrong price, only a computed one or a human one. Every number the automation quotes was consciously entered by the firm at a specific spot in a structure the system owns; anything the firm did not price, at any depth, routes to the firm owner under one law with one enforcement point. Firms that want simple pricing get two flat fees and never see the machinery; firms that want their pricing dialed opt into depth one tier at a time, and their unfinished corners are quote territory rather than errors. The form never knows any of this exists: it renders questions from configuration and ships raw facts, which is also why every lead's real quantities land in the behavioral log in comparable form no matter how differently two firms shaped their pricing. Structure is system-owned, prices are firm-owned, judgment stays human. That split is the whole design.
